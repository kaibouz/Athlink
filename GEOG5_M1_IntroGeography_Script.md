### 2. Defining geography（ノート推奨）

Let's define the discipline of geography. We can use this classic definition of geography that describes the discipline as **the study of the Earth as the home of humans**. This is a very broad definition of geography, but it works pretty well because that is what geographers tend to be interested in. We don't study things too deep into the ocean typically. We don't study things too high up into the atmosphere or outer space typically. Instead, we focus on the realm of Earth — studying the Earth as the home of humans. So this would include the **four environmental spheres** that we'll cover this semester: the **atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, and lithosphere** — and more on those later.

Of course, that first classic definition is still sort of vague, so it helps to have a more specific definition. Here's a more detailed definition of the discipline of geography:

> **Geography is a science that studies the relationships among natural systems, geographic areas, society, cultural activities, and the interdependence of all of these over space.**

I'll admit this definition is still a little vague — you still might not have a good sense for what geographers are interested in studying. So let's go into this in a little bit more detail.

---

### 3. Realms of geography（ノート推奨）

This is a diagram that shows the different realms within geography. Generally speaking, geography is divided into two broad realms or categories: **physical geography**, which of course is what this class is all about, and **human geography**.

**Physical geography** takes the strengths of other sciences — such as oceanography, climatology, geology, soil science, biology, and so on — and looks at how these different sciences connect over space. What are the relationships between climate and vegetation, for example? How do soils develop as a result of climate? How do plants influence the development of soils? How does the underlying geology of the Earth influence the soils and the plants and the animals? Those connections, those relationships — that's what physical geographers are really good at looking at. It would be the natural-science side of geography.

But geography is also a social science, and that is **human geography**. Human geography takes the strengths of other social sciences and humanities disciplines — such as women and gender studies, religious studies, history, political science, anthropology, sociology, and economics — and looks at how those different disciplines connect over space. So in other words: how might a culture's religious practices and beliefs influence the design of its urban areas, the design of its cities? Or how might its political and economic systems have been influenced by gender and history? So human geographers look at how these different human systems connect over space.

Connecting the two realms is what is often called **environmental geography**. This specifically looks at how humans impact the environment.

Lastly, there is also a field within geography called **geospatial models and methods**. These are basically the tools that geographers use to study physical, environmental, and human geography — studying maps and creating maps, using **GIS** (Geographic Information Systems), and so on.

### 4. Example — Physical geography (Banff)

You still might not have a really good sense for what geography is at this point, so I want to use some real-world examples. One of the big takeaways I'm hoping you get from this course is that you will look at the world differently when you're done, as a result of having a better understanding of geography.

Let's start with physical geography. This is a photo of **Banff National Park** in Canada. A physical geographer would note the connections and relationships between several different environmental spheres happening here — biosphere, atmosphere, lithosphere, and hydrosphere.

For example, looking at the vegetation: we see a conifer forest of evergreen trees — maybe spruces, firs, and pines. But up to a certain elevation, the trees stop. We've reached **tree line**. Above that point, the environment is probably not suitable for growing trees — soils too thin, air too cold, winters too long and harsh, maybe too much snow for too long. All of that contributes to where that line of vegetation ends. The fact that it's conifers rather than broadleaf trees like oaks and maples is also influenced by the climate in this mountainous environment.

We can also tell there's been climate change: in the top left you'll see the remains of a glacier — a little left, but not coming down the mountain nearly as much as it used to. Further evidence: the valley in the foreground has a gentle **U-shape**. A U-shaped valley means glaciation — glaciers are essentially big rivers of ice that scoop out the landscape. If the valley were carved by a river, it would be a **V-shape**. So this environment gets cold enough for glaciers, but glaciers no longer extend downslope as much as they used to. Right there: connections among lithosphere, biosphere, climate, and even human impacts on climate.

### 5. Example — Human geography (cemeteries)

Similarly, a photograph that illustrates human geography: a cemetery in **New Orleans, Louisiana**. A human geographer can tell pretty quickly this is a wealthy cemetery — elaborate, large graves require income. Crosses suggest a dominant religion that is some form of Christianity. We can also tell something about climate: graves are above ground. New Orleans / Louisiana cemeteries are known for this because sea level is above the elevation of the city in certain parts, so people (at least those wealthy enough) bury above ground; otherwise the water table and high sea level could disturb gravesites. So from one photo: relatively wealthy Christian community in a climate with high water in soils / high sea level.

Now a cemetery from **Morocco**. We can discern socioeconomic characteristics, dominant religion, and climate. It's much simpler than New Orleans — partly cultural: this is a **Muslim** cemetery, and simplicity in burial is highly valued, so graves are modest. We also notice the neighborhood is probably less wealthy — less extravagant display of wealth. And it's a drier climate, as evidenced by the vegetation. That's how a human geographer might approach these photos.

### 6. Example — Environmental geography (Brazil deforestation) ← いまの画面のイメージ

An environmental geographer would look at this photo of deforestation in **Brazil** and discern a number of relationships. We see a road constructed into the center of the rainforest — deep in the Amazon. It's not a very good road; people probably can't travel it all the time; it may become impassable. If you rely on this road to get products to market or to a hospital, it could be problematic — people dependent on this road are probably dealing with poverty.

This road exists probably because of **tropical deforestation** — huge demand for tropical forest land and products. The road was built hastily to quickly liquidate those assets of the tropical rainforest.

We can also tell something about the soils: reddish / yellowish-reddish color means not a lot of organic material — classic tropical rainforest soils (more later in the course). They're nutrient-poor. If you remove the rainforest and try to farm these soils, they wear out pretty quickly.

### 7. Tools geographers use

Lastly, the tools geographers use — for example, maps.

Satellite imagery: we can measure so many things constantly around Earth thanks to satellites; it takes geographers to interpret and manage that much data. For example, the head of NASA used to be a geographer.

Geographers also use imagery from satellites, planes, and helicopters to help manage disasters. Example: satellite image of a fire in a remote mountainous area — geographers could see where fires were still actively burning and where they had gone out, well before emergency workers could get into those areas.

Geographers also use these tools to look at changes over time. Example: aerial photo from **1937** — forest patch circled, surrounded by rectangular farm fields. Then **1971**, almost 40 years later — forest still there, roads through it, still some farms, but much more suburban development / sprawl.

And the most important tool: **GIS** — Geographic Information Systems — stacking layers (customers, streets, parcels, elevation, land use) to find, for example, the best location for a new store. Jobs in GIS pay pretty well; SMC offers a GIS certificate. Watch the brief Esri intro video posted on Canvas.

Examples of GIS applications: business site selection by customer demographics and roads; environmental management — where endangered terrapins get caught in abandoned crab pots, where to patrol, where to establish a sanctuary.

### 9. Why study physical geography?（ノート任意）

Reasons it's useful even if your career isn't environmental:

1. We're living through tremendous environmental change — climate change, extinctions, challenges with water and soils — affecting all of us.
2. We're dependent on products from around the world, shaped by climate, vegetation, socioeconomics, geopolitics.
3. Many jobs want geographic understanding — e.g. Google hiring a geographic specialist with passion for African geography and mapping; language skills make you more marketable.

Favorite quotes:

- **Mark Twain (1869):** Travel is fatal to prejudice, bigotry, and narrow-mindedness…
- **Maya Angelou:** Travel may not prevent bigotry, but it can introduce the idea that if we try to understand each other, we may even become friends.
- **St. Augustine:** The world is a book, and those who do not travel read only one page.

Why study geography? It's interesting to learn about places and how everything is connected — a perspective important for today's problems. I hope you leave the course looking at the world differently.
