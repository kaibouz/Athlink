# Project Prospectus
## Where Does L.A.’s Water Come From?
### An Independent Public Geography & Cartography Project

**Author:** Kai Nozawa  
**Status:** Independent project (not submitted for course credit)  
**Advisor consultation:** Informal review requested from Dr. Jing Liu, Santa Monica College  
**Primary deliverable:** Public interactive map story (ArcGIS StoryMap or equivalent)  
**Secondary deliverable:** This prospectus + methods/sources documentation

---

## 1. Purpose

This project creates a publicly accessible, academically grounded map story that answers a simple question with a complex geography behind it:

> Where does Los Angeles get its water, and what does that long-distance supply system mean for drought and climate resilience?

The goal is **public geography**: help general audiences understand an invisible urban system, while maintaining transparent methods, sources, and limits suitable for academic review.

This is **not** a course assignment. Techniques from Santa Monica College GIS/cartography coursework (basemap design, projection, thematic symbolization, layout, uncertainty communication, and interactive storytelling) are applied as craft, not as graded requirements.

---

## 2. Research Question

**Primary research question**  
How is Los Angeles’s urban water supply spatially produced through local sources and long-distance interbasin transfers, and what vulnerabilities does that geography create under drought and climate stress?

**Supporting questions**
1. Which major corridors move water into the Los Angeles region?  
2. How should a public map communicate multi-source dependency without false precision?  
3. How can cartography make water security legible to non-specialists?

---

## 3. Significance

### Public significance
Most residents encounter water only at the tap. The infrastructure and source regions that make urban life possible remain abstract. A clear map story can improve geographic literacy about California’s water system.

### Academic significance
The project sits at the intersection of:
- **Water resource geography** (interbasin transfer, metropolitan supply)  
- **Hazards & resilience** (drought exposure, supply diversification)  
- **Cartographic communication** (ethics of simplification, uncertainty, public design)

It contributes as a communication and synthesis project rather than as original hydrologic modeling.

## 4. Study Area & Scale

| Scale | Role |
|---|---|
| Los Angeles / Southern California metro | Where water is consumed (narrative home) |
| Eastern Sierra / Owens corridor | Los Angeles Aqueduct source story |
| Colorado River Basin (selected) | Shared Southwest supply corridor |
| Northern California / Delta / SWP spine | State Water Project corridor |
| Local basins & reuse systems | Groundwater, recycling, capture |

The protagonist of the story is **the L.A. tap**. Statewide context supports that story; the project will not attempt a complete atlas of all California water rights.

---

## 5. Conceptual Framework

1. **Interbasin transfer** — urban water as a spatial product of distant watersheds  
2. **Water security** — reliability, diversity, and stress of supply  
3. **Hydrosocial perspective (light touch)** — infrastructure and institutions shape how “natural” water becomes urban water  
4. **Resilience** — diversification (imports + local sources + demand management) as adaptation

Flood inundation mapping is intentionally out of scope for version 1. The hazard framing here is **supply-side drought and climate risk**, not pluvial flood footprints.

---

## 6. Methods

### 6.1 Cartographic / GIS methods
- Compilation of authoritative public geospatial layers for aqueducts, basins, and regional context  
- Corridor storytelling maps with restrained basemaps and strong visual hierarchy  
- Optional terrain context for mountain source regions  
- Approximate source-contribution communication with explicit uncertainty notes  
- Interactive sequence via StoryMap chapters

### 6.2 Cartographic principles applied
- Basemap subordination to thematic content  
- Appropriate projection choice for California statewide vs. regional insets  
- Sequential/categorical color with accessibility in mind  
- Sparse labeling and balanced layout  
- Honest representation of uncertainty and data limits

### 6.3 Sources (indicative; finalized in Methods chapter of the site)
- Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (LADWP) public materials  
- Metropolitan Water District of Southern California (MWD) public materials  
- California Department of Water Resources / State Water Project documentation  
- USGS / state hydrography and basin layers  
- Esri Living Atlas and other open geospatial layers where appropriate  

All maps will include source citations and access dates.

## 10. Evaluation Criteria (self + advisor feedback)

1. **Clarity of question** — Does a new reader understand what is being asked?  
2. **Geographic accuracy** — Are corridors and source regions responsibly represented?  
3. **Cartographic quality** — Hierarchy, legibility, ethical simplification  
4. **Academic integrity** — Sources, uncertainty, limits  
5. **Public value** — Would a non-specialist learn something durable?

Requested feedback from faculty (non-graded):
- Is the scope appropriate for a strong independent public project?  
- Which water-source datasets are most trustworthy for a public synthesis map?  
- Where is the boundary between helpful simplification and misleading cartography?  
- What would make a Phase 2 (local Santa Monica focus, or participatory layers) most rigorous?

---

## 11. Ethics & Positionality

- Avoid stigmatizing source-region communities.  
- Distinguish documented history from speculation.  
- Do not present approximate supply shares as exact legal entitlements.  
- Cite institutions clearly; avoid implying official endorsement.
