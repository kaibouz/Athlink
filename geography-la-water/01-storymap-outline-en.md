## Chapter 0 — Title & Promise

**Purpose**  
Open with one clear question and one promise: this story traces the long-distance geography behind everyday tap water in Los Angeles.

**On-screen text**  
Where Does L.A.’s Water Come From?  
A public map story of Southern California’s long-distance water supply.

**Visual**  
Quiet hero map of Southern California with faint supply corridors; minimal labels.

---

## Chapter 1 — The Tap in Los Angeles

**Purpose**  
Start local and familiar. Show that the water people drink in L.A. is not only “local rain.”

**Key message**  
Every glass of water in Los Angeles is the end of a much longer spatial journey.

**Map**  
L.A. Basin / city outline; simple locator. Plain or light basemap (GIS 25 Module 1).

**Keep short**  
1 short paragraph + 1 map.

---

## Chapter 2 — A City That Imports Water

**Purpose**  
Establish the big geographic fact: Southern California’s metropolitan water system depends on interbasin transfers.

**Key message**  
L.A. grew by moving water across mountains, deserts, and political boundaries.

**Map**  
California statewide view with three major import corridors highlighted (not yet detailed). Flow-style arrows OK (Module 3 flow mapping ideas).

**Academic cue**  
Introduce term lightly: *interbasin transfer*.

---

## Chapter 3 — Eastern Sierra — Los Angeles Aqueduct

**Purpose**  
Tell the first major supply story: Owens Valley / Eastern Sierra → Los Angeles Aqueduct → L.A.

**Key message**  
One of L.A.’s foundational water sources begins hundreds of miles away in the Eastern Sierra.

**Map**  
Route of the Los Angeles Aqueduct; elevation/terrain basemap optional (Module 7).  
Optional callout: source region communities and landscape change (ethics / historical geography, brief).

**Tone**  
Factual and careful — acknowledge that water transfer has local consequences without turning the whole chapter into activism.

---

## Chapter 4 — The Colorado River Lifeline

**Purpose**  
Second corridor: Colorado River → aqueduct systems serving Southern California (via Metropolitan Water District context).

**Key message**  
L.A.’s water story is also a Southwest story — shared among many states and cities.

**Map**  
Colorado River Basin context + corridor toward Southern California.  
Keep labels few; strong visual hierarchy (Module 6).

**Academic cue**  
Shared river allocation / multi-state dependency / drought exposure.

---

## Chapter 5 — Northern California & the State Water Project

**Purpose**  
Third corridor: Northern California / Delta → State Water Project → Southern California.

**Key message**  
Water that reaches Southern California may begin in watersheds people rarely associate with Los Angeles.

**Map**  
SWP spine southward; Delta as a critical (and contested) node — briefly noted.

---

## Chapter 6 — Local Water — Groundwater, Recycling, Capture

**Purpose**  
Balance the import narrative: local groundwater, recycled water, stormwater capture, conservation.

**Key message**  
Resilience is not only about distant aqueducts — it also depends on local sources and demand management.

**Map**  
L.A. region local layer: basins / recycled water plants (selected) / conceptual capture sites.  
Do not overclaim precision; use clear notes on data limits (Module 5 uncertainty).

---

## Chapter 7 — Drought, Climate, and Resilience

**Purpose**  
Connect supply geography to hazard & resilience without switching the project into a flood map.

**Key message**  
When source regions dry, snowpack shrinks, or shared rivers decline, a city that imports water inherits those risks.

**Map options (pick 1–2)**  
- Side-by-side: “many sources” vs “over-reliance on one corridor” (conceptual)  
- Simple chart + map: approximate contribution ranges by source (with uncertainty note)  
- Timeline note: drought years as stress tests

**Academic cue**  
Water security / supply diversification / climate adaptation.

---

## Chapter 8 — What This Map Asks Us

**Purpose**  
Close for the public: one takeaway, no clutter.

**Key message**  
To understand Los Angeles, follow the water — across mountains, deserts, and decades of infrastructure.

**Optional soft CTA**  
“Explore the sources.”  
(No signup wall; keep public.)

## Chapter 9 — Methods, Limits & Sources

**Purpose**  
Earn academic trust. Required for professor review.

**Include**  
- Data sources (agencies, layers, access dates)  
- Cartographic choices (projection, classification, basemap rationale)  
- Limits (annual shares vary; simplified corridors; not a legal water-rights atlas)  
- Software (ArcGIS Online / StoryMaps / Pro as used)  
- Contact / portfolio line (optional)

---

## Design rules for the whole StoryMap

1. One job per chapter.  
2. Prefer light/plain basemaps when thematic corridors must dominate.  
3. Label sparingly; hierarchy over decoration.  
4. Always show uncertainty where numbers are approximate.  
5. Public language first; academic terms in short asides or Methods.  
6. Keep English and Japanese as separate public versions (or EN primary + JA summary site) — choose one system and keep it consistent.
